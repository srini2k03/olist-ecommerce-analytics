Executive summary: Olist marketplace, Jan 2017 to Aug 2018

Bottom line. Olist more than doubled in a year (Jan–Aug 2018 GMV +140% on the same months of 2017), but growth flattened in 2018 (mean month-on-month −1.0%). Almost nobody buys twice: 3.0% of customers ever do. Further growth has to come from keeping customers. Our analysis points to late delivery as the main controllable reason they do not return.

Scope: 99,441 orders from the public Olist dataset. Figures come from executed SQL and notebooks in this repository. The A/B test in recommendation 4 is simulated, with inputs calibrated to real data.

Recommendations

1. Make "delivered by the promised date" the top customer-experience KPI, and fix it lane by lane. - Finding: late orders average 2.27★, against 4.29★ for on-time orders. Controlling for category, state, basket and value, being late cuts the odds of a better review about 15-fold (odds ratio 0.068, 95% CI 0.063–0.074). - Where: late rates reach 21.4% in Alagoas against 4.5% in São Paulo, and the SP → RJ lane alone accounts for 17.6% of all late orders. - Action: pad promised dates on high-risk north-east lanes. Customers tolerate slow delivery but punish late delivery; arriving 1–3 days late already drops the mean to 3.29★. Renegotiate or switch the SP → RJ carrier. - Size of the prize: if the 6.7% of late orders were reviewed like on-time ones, the platform's mean review would rise by about 0.13★ (6.7% × 2.02★).

2. Run a win-back programme for "At-risk big spenders" before day 365. - Finding: this K-Means segment is 12.4% of customers but 38.6% of revenue (R$ 515 average spend, about 298 days since the last order). It has the lowest mean review of any segment (3.95). - Action: send a personal offer in the category they bought, starting with an apology or credit where their order was late. This is the only segment where paid reactivation clearly pays.

3. Nudge recent buyers to a second order between day 30 and day 90. - Finding: month-1 retention is 0.48%. Of the customers who do return, 68% place their second order within 90 days and 83% within 180 days. - Action: trigger a complementary-category bundle for "Recent mid-value buyers" (31.2% of customers, 31.4% of revenue) inside that window, and measure it against a holdout.

4. Do not launch a R$ 150 free-shipping threshold as designed. Test a better-targeted version, and run tests properly. - Finding (simulated, calibrated to real 2018 data): the threshold lifts the multi-item rate (+1.43 pp, p = 0.006). However, the freight subsidy costs R$ 9.60 per order against about R$ 1.44 of extra GMV, because 26% of orders already qualify. - Action: test a threshold just above typical single-item baskets, or a partial subsidy, and use margin as a guardrail. - Testing discipline: - at 222 orders per day, plan about 9-week tests (a 15% minimum detectable effect); - do not peek: checking 20 times inflates the false-positive rate from 5% to 23%; - read results by week of exposure to strip out novelty effects.

5. Use the churn score only to prioritise cheap retention actions, and prove uplift first. - Finding: repeat purchase is hard to predict. On a later, held-out window, LightGBM reaches ROC-AUC 0.581 and PR-AUC 0.020 against a 1.18% base rate. Logistic regression performs about as well. Still, targeting the top 10% of customers ranked by the model captures 19.4% of next-180-day repeat buyers (1.94x lift), against 15.4% for a "most recent first" rule. - Action: use the score for email and push to the top 10–20%. Run a randomised holdout before attaching discounts.

Caveats

  • The data is observational, so the delivery-to-review link is strongly supported but not experimentally proven.
  • Repeat-purchase signal is weak, with no browsing or marketing data available.
  • Review "sentiment" relies on keyword proxies.
